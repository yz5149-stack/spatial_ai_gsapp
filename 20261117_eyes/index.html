Eyes on the Street: Morningside Heights

How many people can see each spot of the neighbourhood, hour by hour? Walkers come from the real hourly counts of the 116 St–Columbia subway station. Street lights shape where they walk at night and how far they can see. Add lights or a late-night shop and compare. This measures natural surveillance (“eyes on the street”), not crime.

How it works

  1. The place is the final project's 8 m grid of Morningside Heights: OpenStreetMap streets and paths, NYC building footprints with surveyed heights, terrain.
  2. Street lights. New York does not publish where its street lights are. They are modelled: a pole every 30 m along every avenue and street (the city lights all of them), and, if you keep the box ticked, every 45 m along campus and park paths. Each lights fully within 10 m and fades out by 25 m; buildings and trees cast its shadow. Outages are real: 311 reports of street lights out inside the map switch off the nearest pole (the three nearest for “multiple lights out”).
  3. Walkers. Every hour, the people leaving the station (MTA origin–destination estimates) walk to buildings, weighted by floor area, or out of the map; the people entering it (MTA hourly ridership) walk from them. Other walkers, a multiple of the riders that you set, walk between buildings, shops and the map's edges. Everyone takes the cheapest route; at night a dark cell costs more, so lighting moves people.
  4. Eyes on a spot: the expected number of people who can see it at a random moment of the hour. That counts walkers within 30 m in light (10 m in the dark) with a clear line of sight, plus shopkeepers behind their windows while shops are open (assumed 7:00–22:00; a late-night shop you add stays open until 2:00).
  5. Measures. Watched: at least half a person's eyes on the spot on average. Dark and unwatched: at night, light below 35 % and not watched. Walking time unseen: the share of all walking time spent where nobody else is watching.

What it is not

It does not predict crime and uses no crime data. Crime has causes that street geometry does not hold, and police data records where police look as much as where crime happens. An optimiser trained on it would send more surveillance to neighbourhoods that are already over-policed. This tool measures one spatial idea, natural surveillance, so that a community board can compare lighting and street designs. The hard numbers are the subway counts and the 311 reports; the rest are stated assumptions you can change.
